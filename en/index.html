Football produces more data than anyone can read. I close that gap, where others don't look.

Matches, news, call-ups, video: the information is there, but nobody has the time to read it all. Whoever reads a little more of it has an edge over the opponent. It happens in every field where data grows faster than the people meant to read it. I work on it in football, where I can check it.

The reversed funnel

Usually

You start from football and look for a tool: software, a social channel, a course. Technology comes last and bends to what is already done.

How I work

I start from what technology can do today and ask what football actually needs. Then I take it to the pitch and have it checked by the people who coach.

Three proofs, three gaps

Each one closes part of the gap. They are methods and tools you can look at, not promises.

01

Theseus: replace the tasks, not the roles

When a team loses a player, the usual search is for someone in the same role. But the team lives in the tasks that player carried out, zone by zone and depending on the moment. That is relational football: tasks by zone and context, not fixed positions.

Try it: tap a zone, change the moment

↑ Direction of attack

    The same space asks for different tasks depending on the moment. Who carries them out can change: that is what keeps the team itself.

    Tasks by zone and moment

    In possession

    • Defensive third, centre: Offers close to the ball carrier for a short passing line; One player stays behind the ball as a safe option; A player receiving on the half-turn tries to beat the first line of pressure
    • Defensive third, flanks: Moves wide to stretch the opponent's first press; Comes closer to the ball if the teammate is closed down; Holds the line so no space opens behind
    • Middle third, centre: Receives between the lines, between the opponent's midfield and defence; Moves close to the ball carrier to create an overload near the ball; Players not involved cover space in case the ball is lost
    • Middle third, flanks: Gives width to stretch the opponent's line; Swaps positions with the player coming short; Offers a wide option to switch play
    • Final third, centre: Attacks depth behind the defence; Fills the box: near post, far post, penalty spot; One player stays outside the box for the second ball
    • Final third, flanks: Takes on the defender one-on-one when the side is isolated; Overlap or underlap from the nearest teammate; Looks for the low cross or the cut-back

    Ball lost

    • Defensive third, centre: The nearest player presses the ball carrier immediately; The others close the centre and protect the goal; Distances between the lines shrink
    • Defensive third, flanks: Tucks inside to cover depth; Blocks the cross or shows the carrier outside; Calls out the free player
    • Middle third, centre: Immediate first press on the ball carrier; Central passing lines are closed; Players further away recover below the line of the ball
    • Middle third, flanks: Closes the passing line to the weak side; Shows the carrier towards the touchline; Recovers position quickly if the press is beaten
    • Final third, centre: Counter-press by whoever lost the ball; Cuts the exit pass into the centre; If the press fails, the team drops back compact
    • Final third, flanks: Presses inward to close the safe exit; Prevents the switch of play; Tracks back to restore numbers in midfield

    Ball won

    • Defensive third, centre: First pass to the free player, not the marked one; If there is space, carries the ball to draw an opponent; Secures the ball if the pressure is high
    • Defensive third, flanks: Moves wide at once to give a side exit; Attacks the space the opponent left; Offers support to get out of the recovery area
    • Middle third, centre: First pass forward if a teammate is running in behind; The nearest player breaks free to offer an exit; The others support the move so nobody is isolated
    • Middle third, flanks: Runs in behind on the side left open; Stretches the pitch for the counter; Offers a reference point for the switch
    • Final third, centre: Attacks the goal at once, before the defence is set; Players arriving from behind fill the box; Quick decision: shoot, final pass or keep the ball
    • Final third, flanks: Drives diagonally at goal; Cuts inside to open the lane for a runner; Keeps it simple if the overload has gone

    02

    The radar: dated flags you can check later

    Every day it reads public sources on low-visibility leagues and flags players. Each flag stays in a public archive with its date, so anyone can check later whether that name really emerged. The point is not being right: it is making every flag verifiable.

    03

    ScoutDesk: the report done before you leave the ground

    Notes taken during the match become a structured report while you are still in the stand, instead of staying in the notebook until the next day.

    In beta

    How I work

    1. I look for a point where football decides out of habit or lack of time.
    2. I look at what technology can read there, starting from public sources.
    3. I build something small that can be checked: a map, a dated archive, a report.
    4. The coach or decision-maker validates: data proposes, the pitch decides.

    Limits, stated up front

    • Public sources are incomplete and sometimes wrong.
    • Context matters: the same action means different things in different teams.
    • None of these tools replaces the person watching the match. They help them watch better.

    Why

    15 years as a coach on the pitch, 3-4 years of technology applied to football.

    The pitch taught me that decisions are often made with little information and little time. Technology showed me that much of that information already exists: nobody reads it. I work between the two.
